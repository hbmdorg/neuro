# Anesthesia for Aneurysm Clipping
**Note: this protocol is for open surgery in the OR. There is a different protocol for neurointerventional/endovascular aneurysm coiling/embolization.**

![Aneurysm clipping](/assets/img/clipping.png)

## Background

### Intro
Although we typically think of cerebral aneurysm ruptures as catastrophic events (and they often are), cerebral aneurysms present across a broad clinical spectrum. Some patients undergo preventative clipping for an unruptured aneurysm, while others present after rupture with subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH). Among patients with ruptured aneurysms, clinical severity can range from a small SAH with minimal residual deficits to extensive hemorrhage with significant neurological deterioration. Patients will typically have undergone diagnostic cerebral angiography, and some may have had a prior attempt at endovascular coiling.

Understanding the patient’s aneurysm history, prior interventions, and current neurological status is essential for developing the anesthetic plan. These factors help guide assessment of perioperative rupture risk, blood pressure targets, need for pre-induction arterial line placement, and other key anesthetic considerations.

### Aneurysm rupture risk
As anesthesiologists, we can inadvertently increase the risk of aneurysm rupture through changes in hemodynamics, intracranial pressure, and ventilation. Understanding these factors is essential to minimizing avoidable rupture risk. The **PHASES score**, along with patient- and aneurysm-specific characteristics, can help estimate the risk of aneurysm rupture. Features associated with increased rupture risk include larger aneurysm size, posterior circulation or ACA/PCA location, irregular morphology, a narrow neck, recent aneurysm growth, prior rupture, hypertension, and older age, among other factors. Changes in **transmural pressure** — the pressure across the aneurysm wall—may also increase rupture risk. Important contributors include rapid changes in MAP or ICP, significant hypertension or hypotension (particularly abrupt changes in blood pressure), rapid hyper- or hypoventilation, coughing or bucking, and rapid CSF drainage from an EVD. A key anesthetic principle is therefore to avoid abrupt changes in blood pressure, ICP, and ventilation whenever possible.

For additional information on aneurysm characteristics and rupture risk, see the [NIR document with aneurysm information](/protocols/general-nir-aneuryms-info.html).

### Intraoperative Rupture Risk
In addition to the characteristics listed above, the neurosurgon’s ability to obtain proximal control of the aneurysm will also impact the risk of intraoperative rupture and bleeding (ie can they place a temporary clip proximal to the aneurysm?).  You must speak with the surgical team ahead of time about this (thankfully our neurosurgeons tend to give us honest opinions – if they tell you there is a high risk for rupture - believe them and plan accordingly!).

### Be prepared for a ruptured aneurysm
Although uncommon, intraoperative bleeding from a ruptured aneurysm can be catastrophic. Be prepared with blood in the room, a hot line, and reliable, large-bore IVs (or a central line if necessary). Place pacing pads on the patient and have 100mg of adenosine in the OR (to be given in divided doses if needed).

### What to do if the aneurysm ruptures
* As always, call for help!
* Communicate with the surgical team about volume of blood loss and visually monitor the EBL yourself (look over the drape and on the floor, monitor the Neptune, etc).
* Replace lost volume appropriately.
* Consider lowering the MAP to minimize blood loss, but keep in mind that you must also maintain cerebral perfusion (some suggest dropping the MAP to 50).
* Avoid panic-bolusing large doses of pressors – rapid blood pressure spikes will make the situation much worse.
* The surgeons may ask for adenosine (typically ~20mg boluses followed by a rapid IV flush) – momentary blood flow arrest allows them to visualize the ruptured artery and place a clip on it. Although the heart typically recovers from the adenosine, pacing pads should already be on the patient, just in case.
* See the SNACC Intraoperative Aneurysm Rupture algorithm at the bottom of this protocol for additional info.

### Temporary versus Permanent Aneurysm Clips and Blood Pressure Management
**(Hot Topic for board exams!)**
Once the craniectomy is completed and the dura is opened, a significant amount of time will be spent obtaining surgical access to the aneurysm, with the goal of creating a window through with the aneurysm can be well visualized and clips can be placed both proximal to the aneurysm and on the aneurysm itself. During this portion of the surgery, the BP will typically be kept at the patient’s baseline or “low-normal” to minimize aneurysm rupture risk and maintain cerebral perfusion.

Once the surgical team is ready to place the aneurysm clip(s), they may first elect to place a temporary clip on the artery proximal (aka upstream) to the aneurysm. This will block blood flow to the aneurysm and reduce the risk of rupture as they place the permanent clip(s) on the aneurysm; however, it will also block blood flow to all downstream brain tissue supplied by that artery (often the MCA or ACA!). The blood pressure is typically **increased** when a temporary clip is placed in order to maintain collateral blood flow downstream of the clip. Neuromonitoring will keep a close eye on their signals during this time and will alert the surgical team to any changes. If changes occur, the surgical team may remove the temporary clip and/or ask you to augment the blood pressure.

Once the surgical team has placed permanent clips on the aneurysm itself – and are satisfied with their placement – they will remove the temporary clip, which will restore blood flow through the artery.  The blood pressure is typically maintained at a **low-normal** level for this to avoid rupturing the newly clipped aneurysm while maintaining cerebral perfusion. The surgical team will often ask for indocyanine green once the temporary clip is removed – this allows them to see if any blood flows into the clipped aneurysm (the goal is no flow). If there is residual flow, they may replace the temporary clip and/or adjust or place additional permanent clips. It is important to pay attention during this time as they may be focused and forget to tell you when they are placing clips (**helpful tip**: you can watch the clips being placed on the microscope monitor - temporary clips are usually gold and permanent clips are usually silver).

It is helpful to discuss blood pressure goals for permanent and temporary clipping during time out. Check in with the surgeons about new goals if unexpected situations arise during the procedure.

# Anesthetic Management/Plan for Open Cerebral Aneurysm Clipping Surgeries

## Preop Management
### If your patient is in the ICU +/- intubated
* Know current vent settings, any issues with ventilation/CO2, most recent ABG
* Does the patient have an EVD? If so: what is it “open to” (ex “EVD is open at 10”), what have the measured ICPs been, how much CSF have they been draining, has the patient tolerated lying flat?
* Most recent neuro exam + any seizure activity. Recent EEG data?

### Preop labs
* CBC, BMP (especially Na, K, glu, Cr), INR/PTT
* **T+S & Confirmation** + pre-order blood products
* If outpatient, consider: POCT **gluc**, **HCG**

### Preop meds
* Inpatients: review MAR, including pressor/anti-HTN infusions, steroids, anti-seizure meds, etc.
* Outpatients: confirm whether patient took home meds, especially BP meds. Document last doses of anti-seizure meds.
* **Consider**: preop or intraop tylenol, PONV meds (emend)
* **Avoid**: we often avoid versed in neuro patients as lingering effects may affect the postop neuro exam. Avoid scopolamine for similar reasons.
* **Hold**: blood thinners, including aspirin, ACE/ARBs should be held.

### Cardiac
Review EKG and any other cardiac data. Ruptured aneurysms and SAHs can trigger arrythmias, takutsobu cardiomyopathy, and other cardiopulmonary changes.

### Preop neuro exam
Document any abnormalities.

### General
Know about the patient’s aneurysm and risk factors for rupture (ex HTN, prior rupture, location/size/shape of aneurysm, prior aneurysm treatment, PHASES score, etc).

## Intraoperative Management

### Adenosine and pacing pads
Discuss with surgical team whether they will want adenosine and pacing pads. If so, use intraop order set to order 100mg adenosine (will come in 20mg vials) and pick it up from OR pharmacy. Ask the circulator for pacing pads. Place the pacing pads while patient is awake (it’s easier). Ensure you can easily reach the pacing pad plug after positioning (consider connecting it to pacer/defibrillator).

### Indocyanine Green
The nursing team will usually grab this for you, along with keppra and mannitol.

Politely ask the circulator if you need these meds.

### Monitors:
Standard ASA, art line, temp
* Neuromonitoring will have EEG, no need for sedline.
### Access
* **PIVs**: Need good IV access in case of anuerysm rupture! At least two IVs, preferably at least one 16G IV in a location that can be monitored. Consider a central line if unable to get good IV access.
* **Art line**: consider pre-induction art line if risk of aneurysm rupture during induction.
* **Hot line**: be prepared to give blood
* Foley

### Induction + intubation
* Standard induction (prop, lido, fent or remi, low dose roc) - ensure a smooth induction and avoid BP swings!
* Intubation w ETT (do not use an LMA) - tape ETT well.
  * Avoid rapid hyper- or hypoventilation as this will increase risk of aneurysm rupture.
* 2x bite blocks - ensure tongue not between teeth and bite block, ensure tongue not folded over (will cause tongue necrosis), tape bite blocks in place, but do not tape to ETT (will not be able to adjust ETT intraop if they are taped together).


### Pinning and Registration
* Placement of the Mayfield pins (aka “pinning”) is painful and stimulating. Ensure adequate anesthetic depth and pain control prior to pinning. Ask the surgical team to wait if you need to deepen the patient or if the BP is not well controlled. Have propofol in line and be prepared to bolus if needed (an increase in HR will often precede a spike in BP with pinning).
* Registration of neuronavigation often follows pinning. Avoid touching and moving the patient during registration. Ensure the eyes are taped/re-taped after registration.

### Time out + additional meds
* Clarify: ETCO2 goal, BP goals, plans for temporary clips, aneurysm rupture risk, blood in room
* Confirm additional meds: antibiotics, steroids, mannitol, keppra, Indocyanine Green

### Maintenance
Propofol, remifentanil, +/- small amount of sevo

### BP management
* Pressor: norepi is usually the pressor of choice to counteract any bradycardia caused by remi.
* Anti-HTN: have an anti-hypertensive infusion in line and a bolus ready for induction and emergence (typically nicardipine 200mcg/ml). Discuss BP goals with surgical team during time out and throughout the case.

### IV Fluids
Plasmalyte/Normosol is the fluid of choice for neuro cases. Aim for euvolemia and avoid giving excessive IV fluid to minimize risk of cerebral edema.
* Matching UOP volume with plasmalyte/normosol is a good way to maintain euvolemia, especially when mannitol is administered. Please document UOP and IVF ~hourly.
* Consider monitoring PPV, checking lactate and base excess levels to help guide volume management.

### Emergence & Extubation
* Is your patient safe for extubation? Discuss with your attending if unsure. Consider any intraop complications, baseline neuro status, etc.
* Ensure a smooth wake-up (often achieved by running prop/remi until the end of the case).
* Avoid coughing and bucking to prevent aneurysm rupture.
* Avoid HTN: bolus anti-HTN as needed (nicardipine is usually first-line – often 100-200mcg initially).
* Avoid long-acting opioids: consider waking up on remi and then titrating additional pain meds if needed once patient is awake, after the neurosurgical team has completed an initial postop neuro exam. 	
* Ensure a purposeful response from your patient prior to extubation (ex open eyes), but weigh this against the risk of coughing/bucking.

### Additional Intraop considerations
* Intraoperative neuromonitoring will be used.
* See info about temporary clips and BP control above if unfamiliar with these cases.
* See info about avoiding aneurysm rupture if unfamiliar with cerebral aneurysms.
* **Glucose**: Remember to check intraop glucose in patients with potential for hyperglycemia. The risk will be increased when steroids are administered.
* **Postop neuro exam**: The neurosurgical team will want to do a neuro exam after extubation, so avoid long-acting meds that can interfere with mental status (example dexmedetomidine, versed, dilaudid) until after neuro exam is completed.
* **Delayed Emergence**: If the patient is not waking up in a timely manner, follow the SNACC guidelines for delayed emergence (confirm paralytic reversal, check glucose, ABG, temp, and sedline). Discuss a postop head CT with surgical team to rule out stroke, bleed, etc.
* Do **not** order meperidine for neuro patients going to PACU.

### Postop Management
* Patients typically go to ICU postoperatively for frequent neuro exams.
* Be prepared to manage BP during transport (bring pressors and anti-HTN boluses. Bring nicardipine infusion if patient is persistently HTN after emergence.
* Hand-off should include baseline neuro status, intraop events (ex aneurysm rupture, blood products), accurate I/Os, etc.
