# Asleep Craniotomy with Asleep Motor Mapping for Tumor Resection
_The Nitrous Protocol_

## Intro
### Asleep Motor Mapping
**Triple Motor Mapping During Asleep Craniotomies**

Asleep craniotomies for tumor resections are one of the most common neurosurgical procedures that we do.
From a surgical standpoint, the goal is to resect as much of the tumor as possible while limiting damage to functional brain tissue, particularly the motor cortex.
This is achieved using a technique called triple motor mapping in conjunction with neuronavigation and 5-ALA.

Triple motor mapping includes transcranial or transcortical motor evoked potentials (MEPs), bipolar cortical mapping, and monopolar subcortical mapping.
Similar to spine cases, MEPs are performed with corkscrew electrodes in the skull and peripheral needle or sticker electrodes used to monitor descending motor signals.
Occasionally a motor strip will be placed directly on the cortex instead of corkscrew electrodes.
Bipolar cortical stimulation is done using a bipolar probe (aka “The Ojemann”) to deliver an electric current directly to the brain while monitoring for EMG response peripherally.  These two techniques are employed to find a safe entry point through the cortex to access the tumor.

Once safely through the cortex, the surgeons will begin tumor resection.
Throughout resection, they will continue to monitor MEPs, and will use both bipolar and monopolar stimulation to avoid damaging functional brain tissue around the tumor.
Unlike bipolar stimulation, monopolar stimulation sends the electric current down the brain to a grounding pad on the patient’s leg.
If stimulation is in close proximity to subcortical motor tracts, it will elicit a peripheral response.
Once this occurs, the neuromonitoring team will find the minimal amplitude required to elicit the response (aka “the threshold”).
This threshold correlates to distance from the subcortical motor tracts (this is conceptually similar to using electrical stimulation during regional anesthesia).
Being able to estimate this distance allows for maximal tumor resection with minimal damage to surrounding functional brain tissue. 

Why is triple motor mapping relevant to anesthesiologists? There are two primary reasons: because many of our anesthetics can interfere with monitoring and because electrical stimulation to the brain can trigger seizures.  Because the amplitude of the electrical stimulation during monopolar mapping correlates with distance from motor tracts, anesthetics that increase the required amplitude will falsely increase the estimated distance and limit tumor resection.
For this reason, we use nitrous oxide (typically 50-70%) balanced with sevoflurane to achieve 1 MAC as the primary anesthetic during asleep motor mapping.
This is used in conjunction with high dose remi (0.2mcg/kg/m) to minimize the risk of movement or recall.
Although we do not have any blinded studies directly comparing the use nitrous/sevo versus propofol, it has been the experience of the surgeons that nitrous is more favorable. (Author’s note: I have seen a 1-2mA difference when we have compared nitrous/sevo and propofol intraop.
Because resection is typically stopped around 5mA, this may be significant.)

Once resection is complete and the surgical team has closed the dura, a final set of motors will be run by the neuromonitoring team.
At this point, nitrous oxide is turned off to prevent accumulation below the dura, potentially risking tension pneumocephalus.
We typically run prop/remi to finish the case, as this also helps facilitate a smooth wake up and may decrease the risk of PONV.
As noted, electrical stimulation to the brain always carries a risk of seizure.
For this reason, it is important to always monitor the patient for seizure-like movements during mapping, ensure there is propofol in-line, and that there is ice cold saline available in the sterile field.

### Neuronavigation

Neuronavigation with overlaid corticospinal tractography is essentially GPS for brain surgery.
The patient gets a high-resolution MRI the day before their surgery.
The neuroradiology team outlines the functional areas of the brain, including the subcortical motor tracts.
Fiducials are glued to the patient’s forehead area for the MRI and left in place until surgery, and are then used to “register” the patient at the beginning of the case.
Registration tells the system where the patient is in 3D space.
During surgery, a reference array (aka wand) held up to the patient’s brain will show the surgeon where they are relative to the images.
This is helpful in determining extent of tumor resection and proximity to functional brain tissue.  It is important not to move the patient during registration as this can cause interference.

### 5-ALA
5-ALA (Gleolan) may be used to assist with resection of high-grade gliomas, including glioblastomas.
Patients ingest the medication preoperatively; it preferentially accumulates in high-grade tumor cells, which fluoresce under intraoperative microscopy, helping surgeons visualize tumor tissue.
Because 5-ALA causes significant photosensitivity, patients are at risk for severe phototoxic skin reactions (similar to a sunburn), including from standard operating-room lighting.
For patients who received 5-ALA, cover exposed skin with blankets and notify the ICU or PACU team so that appropriate light precautions can be continued postop.

### Intraop Seizure Management
Seizures during general anesthesia are rare, but can occur, especially during asleep motor mapping.
It is important to be prepared to manage them and to communicate well with the surgical team when seizures occur. 
Be prepared: Always keep a propofol bolus and flush syringe in line, particularly during mapping.
Ensure the surgical team has sterile ice-cold saline ready.
Closely observe the patient during mapping and report any seizure-like movements to the team. 
Management of seizures: Immediately inform the surgical team of seizure-like movement If the seizure is focal, ask the surgeons to pour ice-cold saline to the exposed brain and continue until seizure activity resolves.
If it is a generalized seizure (or if a focal seizure generalizes), inform the surgical team, and ask them to use ice-cold saline while you simultaneously bolus ~1mg/kg of propofol, repeating as necessary.
If seizure activity persists, consider midazolam, Ativan, Keppra, and/or fosphenytoin (discuss with surgical team).
Once the seizure resolves, re-assess the patient’s position (particularly the neck) and discuss additional AEDs with the surgical team.

## Setting up the room
1. **Nitrous + Sevo**: Open the nitrous tank behind the anesthesia machine and ensure the pressure in the tank is between ~680-750 psi (if it is <400psi, request a new tank).
Ask the anesthesia techs for help if you are uncertain how to do this.
Also ensure the sevo vaporizer is full.
1. **Infusions**: prop, remi, norepi
   * It is helpful to have the IV pole with the infusion pumps on the same side of the bed as the patient’s feet. Have an empty IV pole at the head of the bed. 
   * Infusion carrier: 1L bag of normosol/plasmalyte as the infusion carrier. Connect it to an octopus (do not use T-pieces). 
1. **Bolus syringes**:
   * Norepi 8mcg/kg
   * Nicardipine 200mcg/ml
1. **Other meds to have ready**:
   * Induction meds (fent, lido, prop, low-dose roc)
   * Ancef, decadron
   * Grab a bag of IV Tylenol from pacu pyxis (save it for closing)
   * The nursing team will grab Keppra and mannitol for you
1. From tech: Art line, ultrasound, hot line, +/-glide
   * Be ready to draw an ABG after starting nitrous but before giving mannitol  
1. Art line supplies, second IV kit
1. Endotracheal tube, airway equipment set up
1. Standard Safety Checks: anesthesia machine, suction, and all other standard safety equipment.

**Room set up note**: Be prepared for a standard induction (fent, lido, prop, low-dose roc).
Following induction, start the nitrous ~50-70% (use your clinical judgement) and sevo to achieve ~ 1MAC, as well as the remi infusion.  Draw an ABG prior to giving mannitol to ensure an adequate PaO2 w nitrous and to check the PaCO2: ETCO2 gradient.
Do not run the FGF low because the FiO2 will already be low.
Following dural closure, we will turn off the nitrous/sevo and switch to prop/remi, so be prepared with a prop infusion.  

## Preop management
In addition to a standard preop evaluation, consider the following:

**Preop labs**
* CBC, BMP (especially Na, K, glu, Cr), INR/PTT
* **T+S & Confirmation** + pre-order blood products
* Consider: POCT gluc, HCG

**Preop meds**
* **Document** last dose of **decadron** + **anti-seizure meds** if taking. 
* Confirm whether patient took other home meds, especially BP meds. 
* **Recommend**: Emend (preop or intraop ok) 
* **Avoid**: no versed in neuro patients as lingering effects may affect the postop neuro exam (discuss with attending if you think patient requires anxiolytic). Avoid preop opioids to minimize hypoventilation/increased ICP. Avoid scopolamine and other centrally acting meds. 
* **Hold**: blood thinners, including aspirin, ACE/ARBs should be held.

**Preop neuro exam**: 
Document any abnormalities.

## Intraoperative Management
**Monitors**:
Standard ASA, art line, temp
* Neuromonitoring will have EEG, no need for sedline.

**Access**
* **PIVs**: 2x PIVs (18-20G usually sufficient, consider larger for large meningiomas)
* **Art line**: typically post-induction
* **Hot line** (use for mannitol)
* Foley

**Induction + intubation**
* Standard induction (prop, lido, fent or remi, low dose roc) - ensure a smooth induction and avoid BP swings!
* Intubation w ETT (do not use an LMA) - tape ETT well.
* 2x bite blocks - ensure tongue not between teeth and bite block, ensure tongue not folded over (will cause tongue necrosis), tape bite blocks in place, but do not tape to ETT (will not be able to adjust ETT intraop if they are taped together).

**Pinning and Registration**
* Placement of the Mayfield pins (aka “pinning”) is painful and stimulating. Ensure adequate anesthetic depth and pain control prior to pinning. Ask the surgical team to wait if you need to deepen the patient or if the BP is not well controlled. Have propofol in line and be prepared to bolus if needed (an increase in HR will often precede a spike in BP with pinning).
* Registration of neuronavigation often follows pinning. Avoid touching and moving the patient during registration. Ensure the eyes are taped/re-taped after registration.

**Time out + additional meds**
* Clarify: ETCO2 goal, BP goals
* Confirm additional meds: antibiotics, steroids, mannitol, Keppra

**Maintenance**
* Initially: nitrous/sevo + remi
* Immediately after dural closure: propofol, remifentanil, +/- small amount of sevo

**BP management**
* Pressor: norepi is usually the pressor of choice to counteract any bradycardia caused by remi. 
* Anti-HTN: Nicardipine 200mcg/ml. Have a 10ml bolus syringe.
* Discuss BP goals with surgical team (typically SBP <140)

**IV Fluids**
Plasmalyte/Normosol is the fluid of choice for neuro cases.
Aim for euvolemia and avoid giving excessive IV fluid to minimize risk of cerebral edema.
* Matching UOP volume with plasmalyte/normosol is a good way to maintain euvolemia, especially when mannitol is administered. Please document UOP and IVF ~hourly.

**Emergence & Extubation**
* Ensure a smooth wake-up (often achieved by running prop/remi at the end of the case).
* Avoid coughing and bucking to prevent intracranial bleeding
* Avoid HTN: bolus anti-HTN as needed (nicardipine is usually first-line – often 100-200mcg initially).
* Avoid long-acting opioids: consider waking up on remi and then titrating additional pain meds if needed once patient is awake, after the neurosurgical team has completed an initial postop neuro exam.
* Ensure a purposeful response from your patient prior to extubation (ex open eyes), but weigh this against the risk of coughing/bucking.

**Delayed Emergence**:
If the patient is not waking up in a timely manner, follow the SNACC guidelines for delayed emergence (confirm paralytic reversal, check glucose, ABG, temp, and sedline). Consider post-ictal state if intraop seizure occurred. Discuss a postop head CT with surgical team to rule out stroke, bleed, etc.

## Postop Management
* Patients often go to ICU postoperatively for frequent neuro exams, but may go to PACU and then neuro step-down unit.
* Be prepared to manage BP during transport (bring pressors and anti-HTN boluses. Consider nicardipine infusion if patient is persistently HTN after emergence, but ensure a carrier is running.
* Hand-off should include baseline neuro status, intraop events (ex seizure), accurate I/Os, etc.
* Never order meperidine for neuro patients.
