# Asleep Craniotomy for Tumor resection without Triple (Asleep) Motor Mapping - TIVA

## Intro
### Asleep Crani w MEPs, SSEPs
Asleep craniotomies for supratentorial tumor resections are one of the most common surgeries we do.
From a neurosurgical  standpoint, the goal is to maximize tumor resection while preserving functional brain tissue.
There are a few ways to do this; the decision of which method typically depends on the proximity of the tumor to motor cortex and subcortical motor tracts as well as surgeon preference. 

Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI) MRI is used preoperatively to identify critical white matter (ex nerve fiber bundles), including corticospinal tracts, as well as tumor proximity to these tracts.
When the tumor abuts subcortical motor tracts, [triple motor mapping (aka asleep motor mapping)](/protocols/triple-asleep-motor-mapping.html) is critical for preventing damage to these tracts as the tumor is being resected, thus we use the nitrous protocol.
However, when there is ample distance from the tumor to these tracts, triple motor mapping may not be necessary.
MEPs and/or SSEPs are often still employed to monitor motor and sensory function during the surgery, thus we are able to run TIVA instead of nitrous.
When the tumor is far enough away from any critical brain tissue that can be reliably monitored, neuromonitoring may not be necessary at all.
In these cases we typically still run TIVA for its ICP-lowering properties, to facilitate an immediate postoperative neuro exam, and a smooth wake up.
I recommend always being prepared for intraoperative neuromonitoring even when you do not see if booked (we are often unable to see the booking). 

**Clinical Example**: _A small 1cm ependymoma located in the right lateral ventricle needs to be resected.
Preop MRI shows the tumor is not adjacent to any cortical or subcortical motor tissue._
_However, the anterior cerebral artery and its branches are located between the cortex and the tumor._
_In order to dissect down to the tumor the surgeons will have to be careful not to damage these arteries._
_They therefore opt to monitor MEPs and SSEPs intraoperatively._
_A change in signal may indicate excessive retraction causing compression of an artery or injury to an artery causing a stroke._

### Neuroanesthesia Goals
**Immediate Postop Neuro Exam**: Our goal is to ensure that the patient is awake enough to participate in a neuro exam immediately after extubation.
Patients who do not emerge from anesthesia in a timely manner must be immediately evaluated for stroke or intracerebral hemorrhage.
The [SNACC (Society for Neuroscience in Anesthesiology and Critical Care) guidelines](https://www.snacc.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/SNACC-Cognitive-Aids-for-Neuroanesthetic-Emergencies.pdf) recommend obtaining a stat head CT while maintaining a sterile operating room in case the patient needs emergent decompression or hemostasis.
For this reason, we typically avoid titrating any long-acting medications, including fentanyl and hydromorphone at the end of the case and instead wake up on remi (immediate postop pain is usually controlled with local anesthetic).
We avoid midazolam, ketamine, and other centrally acting meds (ex scopolamine) for similar reasons.
Remember that patients with brain tumors are often more sensitive to the central side-effects of medications. 

**Avoid Increased ICP, Ensure Adequate CPP and a Relaxed Brain**: Performing a craniotomy on a patient with increased ICP can be dangerous, particularly if the brain herniates out through the craniotomy site.
Herniation can cause sheering of the blood vessels along the bone and also prevents the surgeons from being able to operate.
Patients with space occupying lesions, such as tumors, typically have increased ICP.
We have a few strategies that we can use intraoperatively to relax the brain and decrease ICP, including hyperventilation and mannitol administration.
Maintaining cerebral perfusion in patients with increased ICP is essential and is typically done by maintaining adequate systemic blood pressure.  

**Smooth Wake-Up**: Coughing and bucking during emergence significantly increases ICP and can lead to hemorrhage at the operative site.
Following closure, there is no way to control bleeding without repeating the craniotomy.
Thus, it is important to ensure a smooth wake up.
Ensuring the patient can protect their airway and has purposeful responses is also recommended prior to extubation, which creates a challenging emergence (but also makes these cases interesting!).
This is often best achieved by running propofol and remifentanil until the end of the case and avoiding volatiles, particularly around emergence.    

**Blood Pressure Control**: The neurosurgeons typically want the SBP <140 in order to minimize the risk the bleeding.
If you have concerns about maintaining cerebral perfusion pressure (normally 60-80mmHg), discuss this with the surgeon ahead of time.
It is important to have a pressor and an anti-HTN immediately available (we most commonly use norepi and nicardipine, respectively).
If the SBP sags intraoperatively, be very cautious when bolusing pressors to avoid causing a sudden spike in BP (this can cause the tumor to bleed significantly).
Instead of bolusing, many of us slowly titrate the pressor infusion with the carrier running wide open.
This is a skill worth acquiring during your neuro rotation. 

**Maintain euvolemia**: Postop cerebral edema is a feared complication of neurosurgery.
Maintaining euvolemia with a balanced salt solution (plasmalyte, normosol) helps prevent hypovolemia while minimizing the risk of cerebral edema.
Avoid LR which has more free water.
NS used to be the fluid of choice for neuro patients; however, plasmalyte and normosol have been associated with shorter ICU stays, less hypernatremia, acidemia, and AKIs.  

**Facilitate Neuromonitoring**: Many of our anesthetics can interfere with neuromonitoring.
NMBDs interfere with MEPs, sevoflurane, high dose propofol, and dexmedetomidine can interfere with MEPs and SSEPs.   


## Setting up the room
1. **Infusions**: prop, remi, norepi
   * It is helpful to have the IV pole with the infusion pumps on the same side of the bed as the patient’s feet with an empty IV pole at the head of the bed.  This is not as essential as during an awake case though.
1. **Infusion carrier: 1L bag of normosol/plasmalyte** as the infusion carrier. Connect it to an octopus (do not use T-pieces). 
1. **Bolus syringes**: 
   * Norepi 8mcg/kg
   * Nicardipine 200mcg/ml
1. **Other meds to have ready**: 
   * Induction meds (fent, lido, prop, low-dose roc)
   * Ancef, decadron
   * Grad a bag of IV Tylenol from pacu pyxis (save it for closing)
   * Either order preop fosaprepitant or grab some to give intraoperatively
   * The nursing team will grab Keppra and mannitol for you
1. 2x Bite blocks, 4x Tegaderms (2 for eyes, 2 to reinforce tube tape)
1. **From tech**: Art line, ultrasound, hot line, +/-glide
   * Be ready to draw an ABG after intubating but before giving mannitol  
1. Art line supplies, second IV kit
1. Endotracheal tube, airway equipment set up 
1. Standard Safety Checks: anesthesia machine, suction, and all other standard safety equipment.

## Preop Management
In addition to a standard preop evaluation, consider the following:

**Preop labs**
* CBC, BMP (especially Na, K, glu, Cr), INR/PTT
* **T+S & Confirmation**
* Consider: POCT **gluc, HCG**

**Preop meds**
* **Document** last dose of ((decadron + anti-seizure meds)) if taking. 
* Confirm whether patient took other home meds, especially BP meds. 
* **Recommend**: Emend (preop or intraop ok) 
* **Avoid**: no versed in neuro patients as lingering effects may affect the postop neuro exam (discuss with attending if you think patient requires anxiolytic). Avoid preop opioids to minimize hypoventilation/increased ICP. Avoid scopolamine and other centrally acting meds. 
* **Hold**: blood thinners, including aspirin, ACE/ARBs should be held.

**Preop neuro exam**:
Document any abnormalities.

## Intraoperative Management
**Monitors**

Standard ASA, art line, temp
* Neuromonitoring will have EEG, no need for sedline.

**Access**

* **PIVs**: 2x PIVs (18-20G usually sufficient, consider larger for large meningiomas)
* **Art line**: typically post-induction
* **Hot line** (use for mannitol)
* Foley

**Induction + intubation**

* Standard induction (prop, lido, fent or remi, low dose roc) - ensure a smooth induction and avoid BP swings!
* Intubation w ETT (do not use an LMA) - tape ETT well.
* 2x bite blocks - ensure tongue not between teeth and bite block, ensure tongue not folded over (will cause tongue necrosis), tape bite blocks in place, but do not tape to ETT (will not be able to adjust ETT intraop if they are taped together).

**Pinning and Registration**

* Placement of the Mayfield pins (aka “pinning”) is painful and stimulating. Ensure adequate anesthetic depth and pain control prior to pinning. Ask the surgical team to wait if you need to deepen the patient or if the BP is not well controlled. Have propofol in line and be prepared to bolus if needed (an increase in HR will often precede a spike in BP with pinning).
* Registration of neuronavigation often follows pinning. Avoid touching and moving the patient during registration. Ensure the eyes are taped/re-taped after registration.

**Time out + additional meds**

* Clarify: ETCO2 goal, BP goals  
* Confirm additional meds: antibiotics, steroids, mannitol, Keppra

**Maintenance**

* Prop, remi, +/- low dose sevo
* Continue prop/remi until end of case, please do not switch to sevo

**BP management**

* Pressor: norepi is usually the pressor of choice to counteract any bradycardia caused by remi. 
* Anti-HTN: Nicardipine 200mcg/ml. Have a 10ml bolus syringe.
* Discuss BP goals with surgical team (typically SBP <140)

**IV Fluids**

Plasmalyte/Normosol is the fluid of choice for neuro cases.
Aim for euvolemia and avoid giving excessive IV fluid to minimize risk of cerebral edema.
* Matching UOP volume with plasmalyte/normosol is a good way to maintain euvolemia, especially when mannitol is administered.
Please document UOP and IVF ~hourly.

**Emergence & Extubation**

* Ensure a smooth wake-up (often achieved by running prop/remi at the end of the case).
* Avoid coughing and bucking to prevent intracranial bleeding
* Avoid HTN: bolus anti-HTN as needed (nicardipine is usually first-line – often 100-200mcg initially).
* Avoid long-acting opioids: consider waking up on remi and then titrating additional pain meds if needed once patient is awake, after the neurosurgical team has completed an initial postop neuro exam.
* Ensure a purposeful response from your patient prior to extubation (ex open eyes), but weigh this against the risk of coughing/bucking.

**Delayed Emergence**

If the patient is not waking up in a timely manner, follow the SNACC guidelines for delayed emergence (confirm paralytic reversal, check glucose, ABG, temp, and sedline).
Consider post-ictal state if intraop seizure occurred.
Discuss a postop head CT with surgical team to rule out stroke, bleed, etc.

## Postop Management
* Many patients go to ICU postoperatively for frequent neuro exams, but many also go to PACU and then neuro step-down unit.
* Be prepared to manage BP during transport (bring pressors and anti-HTN boluses.
Consider nicardipine infusion if patient is persistently HTN after emergence, but ensure a carrier is running).
* Hand-off should include baseline neuro status, intraop events (ex seizure), accurate I/Os, etc.
* Never order meperidine for neuro patients.
