# Awake Epilepsy Protocol
## Intro
### Epilepsy Surgery:
Temporal lobe epilepsy is the most common form of focal and drug-resistant epilepsy and is highly responsive to surgical treatment, with up to 70% of patients achieving postoperative seizure freedom.
The epileptogenic zone is often associated with hippocampal sclerosis or other lesions caused by infection, trauma, vascular malformations, or tumors.
Surgical treatment aims to completely remove the epileptogenic tissue while preserving nearby areas responsible for critical functions such as language, and movement.
Extensive preoperative evaluation—including video EEG, high-resolution and functional MRI, and neuropsychological testing—is used to localize the epileptogenic zone and identify functional areas.
Clearly delineated epileptogenic zones away from functional structures may be resected by an asleep craniotomy; however, a staged procedure and/or an awake craniotomy may be required to further localize epileptogenic areas and ensure preservation of functional brain tissue.

A staged procedure involves an initial craniotomy for placement of either subdural grids or strips, or stereotactic placement of depth electrodes.
This is typically done under general anesthesia with or without electrocorticography (ECOG).
Postoperatively, anti-epileptic medications are held and patients are monitored for seizures, the location and spread of which are recorded by the implanted devices.
The spread of electrical activity can then be superimposed onto mapped 3D brain images to visualize the proximity of epileptogenic tissue to other critical brain tissue.
If epileptogenic foci appear in close proximity to eloquent cortex, further testing may be done using the electrodes.
If the epileptogenic zone, for example, appears to cross into language centers, additional language testing can be done with coordinated stimulation of the implanted electrodes to determine whether resection will lead to postoperative language impairments.
If the epileptogenic zone is deemed to be safely resectable, the patient will be scheduled for either an asleep or awake craniotomy.

Despite the extensive preoperative workup, both asleep and awake craniotomies use multiple intraoperative neuromonitoring modalities, along with neuronavigation.
Asleep procedures may employ ECOG, MEPs, SSEPs (rarely), and/or cortical and subcortical motor mapping (link to triple motor mapping).
Awake craniotomies also allow for intraoperative language testing and additional motor and sensory testing.
Many anesthetic agents interfere with these intraoperative testing modalities, particularly ECOG, so it is essential that the anesthetic plan be tailored to maximize intraoperative testing reliability.

### Intraop Seizure Mgmt

**Seizures**: We most commonly see focal seizures intraop; however, GTCs can also occur. They are often triggered by mapping. Patients hold their AEDs preop to enhance ECoG recordings, which increases their risk of a periop seizure.
* **Be prepared**:
   * Always keep a propofol bolus and flush syringe in line, particularly during mapping.
   * Ensure the surgical team has sterile ice-cold saline ready.
   * Closely observe the patient during mapping and report any seizure-like movements to the surgeons.
   * Have airway equipment prepared and ensure the circuit can reach the patient.
* **Management of focal seizures**:
   * **Ice-cold saline**: immediately inform the surgical team of seizure-like movement and ask them to pour ice-cold saline to the exposed brain. Continue until seizure activity resolves.
   * Continuously communicate the seizure’s progression/status to the surgical team, who have limited visibility of the patient.
   * If the seizure generalizes, inform the surgical team and bolus ~1mg/kg of propofol, repeat as necessary. Be prepared for apnea and immediate airway management.
   * Once the seizure resolves, re-assess neck and extremity positioning and discuss additional AEDs with the surgical team.
* **Management of Generalized Seizures**:
   * Notify the surgical team, request ice cold saline, and administer ~1mg/kg propofol; repeat as needed.
   * Be prepared for apnea and immediate airway management.
   * If seizure activity persists, consider midazolam, Ativan, Keppra, and/or fosphenytoin. Discuss with surgical team.
   * After seizure resolution, reassess patient positioning and discuss addional AEDs.
* *Additional thoughts*:
   * Early intervention of focal seizures decreases the risk of generalization; maintain close observation of patient throughout mapping.
   * The ECoG and/or neuromonitoring EEG may help confirm seizure activity when the clinical diagnosis is unclear.
   * ECoG may not see a seizure if the grid is far enough away from the seizure.
   *   For focal seizures, prioritize ice-cold saline and avoid unnecessary propofol, as propofol may cause apnea and sedation that can interfere with subsequent awake testing.
   * Keep the pulse ox tone audible throughout the awake portion of the case, particularly during mapping, in case seizure management and/or propofol administration is required.
   * Remain calm and reassuring; seizures can be distressing for awake patients.

### Airway emergencies
Airway emergencies during awake craniotomies can occur due to apnea from sedation, seizures, aspiration, venous air emboli, pulmonary emboli, etc.

* **Be prepared**: when setting up the room always have the following ready:
   * Nasal trumpets + lube (have size 30, 32, and 34 for most adults)
   * Oral airways + tongue depressor
   * LMAs in appropriate sizes + lube + tongue depressor
   * Prepared ETT (stylet, 10ml syringe), NMBD, glidescope + fiberoptic in room.
   * Ensure the circuit is extended and easily reaches the patient.
   * Suction
* **Sedation**: Use extreme caution with sedation once the patient is in Mayfield pins. Avoid boluses of propofol, opioids, or other sedatives. Awake cranis can be performed entirely awake; do not rush sedation. If the ETCO₂ waveform decreases or disappears, immediately stop sedation before hypoxemia develops.
* **Positioning**: Before pin placement and registration, confirm the head is in a semi-sniffing position with a patent airway and adequate spontaneous ventilation. Ensure the position permits emergency LMA placement.
* **LMA**:  An LMA is generally easier to place than an ETT in an emergency. Confirm adequate positioning for LMA placement before securing the pins.
* **Intubation**:  Intubation with a glide or fiberoptic is possible but difficult with the patient in pins. If necessary, the pins can be disconnected to facilitate head positioning for airway access. This is a “worst case scenario” though.
* **Monitoring**: Consider a stopcock on the ETCO₂ sampling line to allow rapid switching between nasal cannula and circuit sampling.
* **Remifentanil**: titrate cautiously. Pump adjustment errors lead to apnea (0.02 versus 0.2 mcg/kg/m).
* **Vigilance**: Continuous attention to ventilation and sedation is the best prevention of an airway emergency.

## Setting up the room
 * **Infusions**: precedex, prop, remi, norepi, nicardipine
   * **Precedex and remi will be the primary infusions for the beginning of this case**, prop can be used after ECoG is complete.
   * **Ensure that the IV pole with the infusion pumps will be on the same side of the bed as the patient’s feet.** Have an empty IV pole at the head of the bed.
 * **Infusion carrier: 1L bag of normosol/plasmalyte** as the infusion carrier. Connect it to an **octopus** (do not use T-pieces).
 * **Bolus syringes**:
   * 10ml syringes of propofol – have 2-3 of these (please use 10ml syringes, do not make 20-30ml bolus syringes)
   * Norepi 8mcg/kg
   * Nicardipine 200mcg/ml, labetalol
 * **Other meds to have ready**:
   * Ancef, decadron, zofran
   * Bag of IV Tylenol (save it for awake portion)
   * The nursing team will grab Keppra and mannitol for you
 * From tech: Art line, ultrasound, hot line, glide + blade + stylet
 * Art line supplies, second IV kit
 * Nasal canula + 2x tegaderms to tape it to the face
 * Emergency Airway supplies:
   * Place on anesthesia machine:
     * Nasal trumpets + lube (have size 30, 32, and 34 for most adults)
     * Oral airways (red and yellow)
     * Tongue depressor
   * Have out/readily available:
     * LMAs in appropriate sizes + lube + tongue depressor
     * Prepared ETT (stylet, 10ml syringe), NMBD, Glidescope + fiberoptic in room.
       * Have NMBD ready, but use tourniquet to tie it to ETT or put it in the pyxis drawer – don’t make it easy to grab by accident.
   * Ensure the circuit will easily reach the patient (add extension if needed)
     * Add a stopcock to the ETCO2 sampler on the anesthesia machine so you can connect both the circuit and the nasal canula (open stock cock to the nasal canula).
 * Check: anesthesia machine, suction, and all other standard safety equipment.

## Preoperative Management
In addition to a standard anesthesia preop eval, things to consider...

**Preop labs**: 
  * CBC, BMP (especially Na, K, glu, Cr), INR
  * T&S + confirmation (typically do not pre-order blood)
  * Consider: POCT gluc, HCG

**Preop meds**: 
 * **Hold home antiepileptics!** We want the patient to have some ictal activity on ECoG, so AEDs are held.
   * Document patient’s home AED regimen and last doses.
 * **Recommend**: Preop **emend**
   * Instead of giving preop tylenol, have a bag in OR to give during awake testing.
 * **Avoid**: **do not give versed** – *it will interfere with ECoG*. Avoid scopolamine, opioids, or other centrally-acting meds.
 * **Hold**: blood thinners, including aspirin, and ACE/ARBs

**Preop neuro exam**:
* Document patient’s seizures: aura (ex lip smacking), focal vs GTC, duration, frequency, post-ictal symptoms. This information will help you identify if patient is having a seizure intraoperatively.

**Cardiac**: review EKG and any other cardiac data.

**Reading glasses**: If patient has reading glasses, bring them to OR.

**Consent**: Take your time in preop so patient gets to know you, talk them through entire procedure, and reassure them that they will be able to communicate with you during the procedure.

## Intraoperative Management
 
**Monitors**: Standard ASA, art line, temp. No sedline.

**Access**: 
 * **PIVs** x2. Ensure one IV is on right (contralateral) hand – this will be for infusions and mannitol. Must be able to monitor for infiltration. The other IV can go on the ipsilateral (left) hand. 18-20G IVs are sufficient.
 * **Art line**: Preferably on left/ipsilateral hand  
 * **Hot line**: for mannitol
 * **Foley**: ensure patient adequately sedated for foley placement, monitor breathing

**Pinning and Registration**: 
 * Neurosurg will inject local anesthetic at the pin sites prior to placing pins, which can be painful, so be prepared to support patient. **Do not bolus propofol or opioids for pinning!** It is important to know whether the local anesthetic is adequate to prevent pin pain before securing the pins.
 * Ensure the head positioning does not obstruct airway (good ETCO2 curve) and that you can place an LMA in an emergency.
 * Neuronavigation registration follows pinning - avoid touching/moving patient

**Scalp Block**: 
 * Neurosurg will do a large field block. Often painful for patient, be prepared with adequate sedation, but maintain good ventilation. Ensure patient does not move arms/legs.

**Time out + additional meds**: 
 * Clarify BP goals + confirm additional meds: antibiotics (usually cefazolin), decadron dose, mannitol, Keppra (typically held)
 * Tell neurosurg if you will need extra time to wake up patient (ex if requiring a high precedex dose)

**Maintenance**: 
 * Precedex, remifentanil (do not use propofol until after ECoG completed)
 * If patient not tolerating sedation, wake them up. Okay to do procedure fully awake.

**BP management**: 
 * Pressor: norepi (avoid bolusing to prevent BP spikes, use gradual infusion titrations)
 * Anti-HTN: nicardipine infusion + bolus syringe, 200mcg/ml + labetalol bolus syringe

**IV Fluids**:
 * Plasmalyte/Normosol is the fluid of choice for neuro cases – use a 1L bag for infusion carrier and keep carrier running when infusions are on.
 * Aim for euvolemia: avoid excessive IV fluid to minimize risk of cerebral edema. Matching UOP with IVF is a good way to maintain euvolemia, especially when mannitol is administered. Monitor UOP and IVF ~hourly. 

**Emergence**:
 * Usually keep sedation running until pins are off
 * Maintain SBP goal (usually SBP<140)

## Postop Management: 
 * Most patients go to ICU, some go to PACU/Stepdown
 * Maintain SBP goal during transport (nicardipine boluses or infusion – ensure carrier running if using infusion)
 * Standard PACU orders are sufficient – do NOT order meperidine for neuro patients!
 * Hand-off should include baseline neuro status, seizure description, and any intraoperative neuro changes.
