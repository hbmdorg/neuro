# Asleep DBS in MRI Protocol

## Intro
Deep brain stimulators are implanted devices that deliver controlled electrical pulses to targeted neural circuits deep in the brain. They typically consist of one or two electrodes (leads) precisely positioned within targeted brain regions, connected via extension wires to a pulse generator implanted below the clavicle, similar to a cardiac pacemaker. DBS is FDA-approved for Parkinson’s disease, essential tremor, dystonia, drug-resistant epilepsy, and treatment-resistant obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD).

DBS implantation can be performed awake with intraoperative testing, asleep with O-arm stereotactic imaging or intraoperative CT, or asleep with intraoperative MRI guidance. Technique selection depends on factors such as the patient’s ability to tolerate an awake procedure, MRI eligibility, and surgeon preference. Current evidence suggests that clinical outcomes are generally comparable between awake and MRI-guided approaches.

For MRI-guided DBS, the patient is anesthetized in the induction room and transferred to the MRI scanner. An initial brain MRI is obtained, after which the MRI table is advanced so that the patient’s head extends out of the far end of the MRI tunnel (so as to be accessible to the surgical team). A burr hole is created, and an MRI-compatible aiming device is used to align the DBS electrodes with the intended target, such as the subthalamic nucleus. Serial MRI scans and image-guidance software are then used to refine the trajectory. Once optimal alignment is confirmed, the electrodes are inserted and final imaging is performed to verify placement. The burr holes are closed, and the patient is returned to the induction/emergence room for recovery from anesthesia. Of note, the pulse generator is not inserted on the same day as the lead placement in MRI-guided cases (unlike cases in the operating room in which it is placed during the same surgery). Patients will come back about a 1-2 weeks after their MRI-guided surgery for the generator insertion. 

Complications during DBS surgery are uncommon, thankfully. For the anesthesiologist, the most important serious complication is intracranial hemorrhage, the risk of which increases with hypertension during electrode placement; we therefore typically target an SBP <130–140 mmHg.  Other intraoperative risks include stroke and seizures. Strict paralysis is essential during these surgeries to prevent injury from movement in pins or during electrode placement. Because there is no TOF monitor in MRI, we often run remi to minimize the risk of movement.

MRI safety is paramount during these procedures. There are many people moving in and out of the MRI room while the patient is in the scanner, including the anesthesiologist. Ferromagnetic objects become dangerous projectiles, so remove all items from your pockets and do not bring cell phones, watches, stethoscopes, ID badges, scissors, anesthesia equipment, bags, or other unsecured equipment into the scanner room. Remove all jewelry and non-MRI-compatible equipment from the patient to prevent RF-related burns, and place earplugs once the patient is anesthetized to protect against MRI noise. Always perform a thorough safety check before entering the scanner room and remain vigilant throughout the procedure - remember that patient safety is **your** job!

## The Flow
Preop →  Induction Rm →  MRI → Emergence Rm → PACU
* **Preop**: patients will start their day in preop on the 4th floor. 
* **MRI Induction Room**: Once ICANDOs are done, you will transport the patient to the MRI induction room on the third floor.
  * MRI 4 = the solo MRI room in the NW corner of the 3rd floor (~ under OR 10)
  * No preop versed. The MRI team will either do a safety check in preop or upon arrival to induction room (patient must be awake).
  * **Remove everything from your pockets upon arrival to the MRI induction room!!** Phones, ID badges, stethoscopes, etc. Do not wait until after induction to do this as you will be focused on getting the patient situated in the MRI scanner.
* **Events in the induction room**: place patient on MRI monitors, induction, intubation, place second IV. Nurse will place a foley and MRI team will place ear plugs. Surgical team will shave the head and then you will move the patient to the MRI bed. The pins are attached to the MRI bed, so once the patient is moved over, they will immediately pin the head (ouch, bolus!). A surgical time out will be done followed by a surgical scalp block. An MRI safety time out will then be done prior to moving the patient to the MRI machine. 
* **Move the patient to the MRI room**: Connect patient to the MRI anesthesia machine, connect IV infusions. Ensure the vent circuit has extension tubing and that it is fully extended. Ensure two ETCO2  sampling lines are untaped/untangled. You will need the full length of these. 
  * The MRI tech will advance the patient into the MRI tunnel until their head sticks out of the far end of the tunnel to ensure all the tubing/monitors etc are long enough. 
  * A very brief MRI imaging check will then be done (you must either stay in or out of the room for this, don’t open the door). After this, a longer round of imaging will be done (~20mins). 
  * Give antibiotics after this first round of imaging (verify timing with surgical team). 
  * Surgical team will then make incision + burr hole(s) and line up the head frame and leads for placement. Multiple additional rounds of imaging and planning will be done with images until surgical team is confident in their placement trajectory. Leads will then be placed (keep SBP <130-140, discuss with surgeon). Following placement, the burr hole will be closed and a final 10-15m MRI scan will be done. 
  * Towards the end of the final imaging, move your personal belongings over to the emergence room.
* **Emergence and PACU**: Once the final imaging is done and the dressing is placed, move the patient from the MRI machine to the emergence room. Switch back to non-MRI monitors for PACU, emerge, extubate, and transport patient to PACU on fourth floor (or ICU if indicated). 

## Setting up "the room”
**Remember**: Always check with the MRI Tech before entering the MRI room. Always double check your pockets, wrists, waistband, head, chest, etc for phones, watches, hair clips, stethoscopes, glasses, jewelry, etc before entering the MRI room. 

1. **Infusion tubing**: You will need at least **350 inches** of infusion tubing **for each infusion** (make sure to **untangle** it before bringing the patient to MRI!)  You can use a T-piece at the patient-end of the infusions, but do not try to connect them close to the syringe pump with a shared carrier. 
   * **The math**: 350in of infusion tubing holds about 30ml of fluid. A 16mcg/kg norepi infusion will run at approx 20ml/hr for a 70kg patient. Therefore, it will take >1hr for any dose titrations to reach the patient if shared carrier tubing is used). 
   * **Note**: You need more infusion tubing for the DBS cases than other MRI cases because the patient is much farther down the MRI tunnel than any other MRI. 
   * **Before bringing the patient to the MRI**, set up the infusion pumps **outside** of the MRI room and run the tubing through the gap in the door. **Untangle** the infusion tubing (350in of tubing comes very pre-tangled!!!), walk it into the MRI room, and hang it on the MRI machine so that it is ready to be connected as soon as the patient is brought into the MRI room.  
1. **Anesthesia machine**: Ensure the anesthesia machine in the MRI room and the induction room were both checked and ensure both sevo vaporizers are full.
   * Ensure the circuit has an extension and that the tubing is fully extended (you will need the full length)
   * Ensure there are two ETCO2 sampling lines and they are untangled/untaped. 
1. Any meds drawn up ahead of time should have plastic caps on them - do not use blunt tip syringes as caps.

## Preop Management
In addition to a standard anesthesia preop eval, things to consider…

**Preop labs** 
* CBC, BMP (especially Na, K, glu, Cr), INR/PTT
* T&S is typically not necessary  
* Consider: POCT **gluc**, **HCG**

**Preop meds:**
* **Parkinson’s Medications**: ensure the patient took their AM dose of PD meds (ie carbidopa/levodopa). Document the time of their last dose as well as their usual dose and frequency. Consider if they need an additional dose prior to rolling back or if they will need one upon arriving to PACU. Discuss with surgeon if you are unsure. (Note of clarification: we usually hold carbidopa/levodopa in awake DBS cases, but patients should take it before asleep cases.)
* **Recommended**: Preop **emend** and **tylenol** 
* **Avoid**: we often **avoid versed** in neuro patients as lingering effects may affect the postop neuro exam. Avoid scopolamine for similar reasons. **Avoid dopamine antagonists in PD patients**, including anti-emetics (avoid metoclopramide, droperidol, haldol, phenothiazines like promethazine).  
* **Hold**: blood thinners, including aspirin, ACE/ARBs should be held.

**Check the preop BP**: Intracerebral hemorrhage during lead placement is a feared complication of DBS surgery. The risk is significantly increased if the patient is hypertensive. The ‘guidelines’ recommend treating HTN prior to rolling to the MRI; however, in my experience, PD patients are prone to hypotension intraoperatively due to autonomic instability. If the patient is hypertensive in preop, use your clinical judgement and discuss with your attending about whether to treat it preoperatively. Is it chronic? Ensure the patient can safely maintain SBP <130 intraoperatively prior to going to the MRI. Discuss any concerns with surgeon prior to going to the MRI. 

**Cardiac**: review EKG and any other cardiac data. 

**Autonomic symptoms**: Review any history of hypotension, dizziness, syncope as this can be common in PD patients.  

**Preop neuro exam**: document any abnormalities  

## Intraop Management

**Monitors**: Use the MRI-compatible monitors, including EKG, BP cuff, and pulse ox. Ensure the MRI monitor has FiO2, ETO2, FiCO2, ETCO2, agents (sevo), and MAC turned on, including alarms, when you connect patient to vent in MRI room (this is a standard ASA requirement!). 
* Ensure any preop monitors/stickers are removed (especially pulse ox stickers, EKG stickers, and BP cuffs with metal on them)
* We are not set up to monitor temperature, TOF, or sedline in the MRI scanner. Place blankets on the patient during surgery and check a TOF after moving back to induction/emergence room. 
* The anesthesia machine in the MRI scanner does not associate with the anesthesia chart. Manually enter data including: VCV, Vt, RR, FGF, Ppeak. FiO2, ETCO2 will automatically pull from vitals monitor.   

**Access**: 
* **PIVs: 2x PIV**. You cannot place an IV in the MRI room, so place a backup IV during induction. Consider a foot IV: You will have access to a foot IV but not the hands/arms during the surgery. 
* **Art line**: not typically placed (but safe to have one if needed – must place it prior to entering MRI room). 
* **Non-temp Foley only**: ensure there are no metal clips on foley prior to entering MRI.

**Induction + intubation:** 
* Standard induction (fent, lido prop, roc) – then typically just run sevo until entering MRI room. 
* Intubation w ETT (do not use an LMA) - tape ETT well and **tape pilot balloon to ETT** so it does not interfere with imaging. 
* **Ensure the laryngoscope blade and stylet are placed in the “yuck bucket” and not left with the patient as they are ferromagnetic.**
* Consider placing a bite block prior to going into MRI room

**Pinning:** 
* One you are ready; the surgical team will help move the patient to the MRI bed. The bed has pins attached to it, so once you move over, you will immediately start pinning. Ensure you can reach the infusions to bolus propofol (remember, you will not be running remi, so the patient **will need** a bolus to prevent HTN during pinning).

**Time out + additional meds:** 
* Standard time out + MRI safety time out: be ready to state where the laryngoscope blade, stylet, and IV needles are – ensure they are not on the patient.
* Clarify: ETCO2 goal (usually normal), BP goals (usually <130-140 during lead insertion), and fluid goals (usually want 1L fluid bolus early to prevent brain sagging).
* Confirm additional meds: antibiotics, (usually cefazolin given after initial round of imaging), steroids (low dose decadron if needed for PONV).


**Maintenance:** 
* Propofol or sevo, remifentanil, norepi  
* Remember that each infusion will need ~350 inches of infusion tubing!

**Pressor/BP management:** 
* Pressor: norepi is usually the pressor of choice to counteract any bradycardia caused by remi. 
* Anti-HTN: have an anti-hypertensive bolus ready for induction and emergence (typically nicardipine 200mcg/ml). 
* Surgeons typically ask for SBP <130-140 during lead placement. Clarify during time out.

**IV Fluids:**
* Plasmalyte/Normosol is the fluid of choice for neuro cases.  
* “Keep the tank full” – we usually give about ~1L fluid bolus at beginning of case to help prevent brain sagging intraop.

**Emergence & Extubation:** 
* Move your phone and any other personal belongings to the emergence room, then transport the patient to the emergence room. Do not bring your phone in the MRI room! 
* Ensure a smooth wake-up, avoid coughing/bucking (running remi until the end of the case helps with this). 
* Avoid HTN: bolus anti-HTN as needed (nicardipine is usually first-line – often 100-200mcg initially).
* Avoid long-acting opioids: can titrate pain meds once patient is awake and a neuro exam has been done. 	
* Ensure a purposeful response from your patient prior to extubation (ex open eyes), but weigh this against the risk of coughing/bucking. 
* Note: **PD patients may have difficulty opening their eyes** if their PD meds are delayed, but they should be able to squeeze your hand.


## Postop Management 
* Most patients go to PACU
* Standard PACU orders are sufficient
* Do not order meperidine for neuro patients
* Handoff to PACU or ICU must include a description of the patient’s baseline neuro status
* Please alert the neurosurgical team and pacu team if the patient is due for parkinson’s medications
