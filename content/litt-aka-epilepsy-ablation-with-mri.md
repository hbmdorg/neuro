# LITT Anesthesia Protocol
Aka Epilepsy Ablation with MRI

## Background
Epilepsy is one of the most common neurological disorders worldwide and is associated with substantial morbidity and impact on quality of life. Approximately one-third of patients with epilepsy develop drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE), defined as persistent seizures despite adequate trials of two or more appropriately selected and tolerated anti-seizure medications (ASMs). Patients with DRE should be referred early for comprehensive evaluation at a specialized epilepsy center, including consideration of neurosurgical intervention.

Seizures may originate from one or more epileptogenic foci within the brain and may remain localized, resulting in focal seizures, or propagate to involve both cerebral hemispheres, resulting in bilateral or generalized seizure activity. Accurate identification and surgical resection of the epileptogenic focus can significantly reduce seizure frequency and, in appropriately selected patients, may result in complete seizure freedom. For patients who are not candidates for resection or who prefer to avoid an open craniotomy, minimally invasive ablative procedures represent an alternative treatment option.

MRI-guided laser ablation is one such minimally invasive surgical technique that enables precise targeting and ablation of epileptogenic tissue. These procedures are performed in a hybrid operating room–MRI suite designed to facilitate seamless transition between the OR and MRI environment. The two areas are separated by a large metal (“garage”) door that serves as a physical barrier between the MRI and OR.

The initial portion of the procedure takes place in the OR. Following induction, intubation, and pinning, the neurosurgical team creates a burr hole through which the laser probe is stereotactically inserted and positioned at the targeted epileptogenic tissue. Once the probe is appropriately positioned, the patient’s monitors are swapped to MRI-compatible monitors. A comprehensive safety check is then performed by the entire operating room team to confirm that all equipment and objects entering the MRI environment are MRI-compatible.

Following completion of the safety check, the dividing door is opened, and the patient is transferred into the MRI scanner. The patient is disconnected from the OR anesthesia machine and connected to the MRI anesthesia machine. Initial MRI sequences (images) are obtained to confirm accurate probe placement and ensure appropriate targeting of the intended tissue. Once satisfactory positioning is confirmed, MRI-guided laser ablation is performed.

After completion of the ablation, the patient is transferred from the MRI scanner to the adjacent induction/emergence area. The neurosurgical team closes the burr-hole incision, after which the patient is awakened from general anesthesia and extubated. The patient is then transported to the ICU for close neurological monitoring.

## **Safety**
MRI safety is of the utmost importance for these procedures and the anesthesia team must ensure that they do not leave any ferromagnetic/metallic objects/monitors/etc on the patient, nor do we bring any ferromagnetic objects into the MRI room. The risk of injury to the patient from an object flying into the scanner or burn injuries from heating while in the scanner are significant and **should never happen**. The MRI team may be understaffed for these procedures, so do not assume someone will be monitoring your movements in/out of the MRI room. Double/triple-check yourself and your pockets before entering the MRI room. Quadruple check the patient before moving them to the MRI scanner. Plan ahead to have infusion pumps ready for both the OR portion and the MRI portion (infusion pumps cannot be brought through the MRI room). And remember: the **MRI magnet is always on**, even when there is no scan in progress, so the risk is always present.

> The MRI is the most dangerous place in the hospital.

## Preop Management
### Preop labs
* Review preop CBC, Na, K, Cr, Glu, INR.
* T&S: Consider a T&S, but no need to order blood products.
* On day of surgery, consider: POCT **gluc**, **HCG** (patients are often reproductive-age)
### Preop meds
* **Anti-Epileptic Meds**: patients should take their scheduled anti-epileptic medications before surgery. Document their last dose and inquire when their next doses are due.
* Consider: **Emend**, **Tylenol**
* **Preop versed**? Although we often avoid preop versed in neuro cases, you can consider it for these patients. Use your clinical judgement: avoid giving versed if you think it may affect the postop neuro exam; consider giving it if the stress of going to the operating room may trigger a seizure.
* **Hold**: blood thinners, including aspirin.

### Seizure history
**In the preop note**, document a description of the seizures, as well as the frequency and date of last seizure. Knowing the frequency of seizures will give you sense of the risk of your patient having a periop seizure. Knowing what the seizures look like will help you identify whether your patient is having a seizure.

Description of seizure should include: any auras (ex lip smacking, anxiety, tingling), what the seizure looks like (ex focal shaking, generalized shaking, blank stare, aphasia, etc); average duration of seizures (ex 1-2 mins); post-ictal symptoms (ex aphasia, fatigue, paralysis, etc).

### Preop neuro exam
Document any abnormalities

### Preop MRI Clearance
Ensure the MRI clearance has been completed and signed by patient prior to giving any anesthetic medications (including versed!).

## Intraop Management
### Monitors
**REMEMBER that you will use one set of monitors in the operating room and then switch to the MRI monitors. The OR monitors and stickers are NOT MRI compatible!**

* **OR Monitors**: standard ASA, temp, TOF (can place post tibial if turning 180 deg).
* **MRI monitors**:
  * EKG, BP, pulse ox
  * Double check that you REMOVED the following OR monitors prior to entering the MRI: the EKG leads AND stickers, the pulse ox sticker, temp probe, TOF monitor, and any other additional monitors you may have placed (ex sedline).
  * You may have to swap out the BP cuff depending on the MRI monitor.
  * You will not be able to monitor temp, TOF, or sedline in the MRI
* Ensure the MRI monitor displays ETCO2, FiCO2, FiO2, ETO2, insp/exp sevo, MAC, and that there is an audible alarm turned on for these variables (this is a standard ASA requirement!).
  * MRI monitor MAC value does not correct for patient's age.

### Access
* **PIVs**: 2 PIVs (18-20G sufficient). The bed is usually turned 180 degrees, so consider running infusions through a foot or saphenous IV that you can monitor intraop.
  * You CANNOT place an IV in the MRI room. We typically place a second IV in the OR, at the beginning of the case, as a backup.
* Art lines are typically not necessary for these procedures
* **+/- foley** depending on expected length of case

### Induction + intubation
* Standard induction (ex prop, roc, lido, fent or remi)
* Intubation w ETT (do not use an LMA) - tape ETT normally (remember you will be transferring patient to MRI and then emergence room, so ensure the ETT is secured well).
  * The pilot balloon of the ETT will need to be taped to the ETT for the MRI to prevent interference with the imaging.

### Pinning and Registration
* Placement of the Mayfield pins (aka “pinning”) is painful and stimulating. Ensure adequate anesthetic depth and pain control prior to pinning. Ask the surgical team to wait if you need to deepen the patient or if the BP is not well controlled. Have propofol in line and be prepared to bolus if needed (an increase in HR will often precede a spike in BP with pinning).
* Registration of neuronavigation often follows pinning. Avoid touching and moving the patient during registration. Ensure the eyes are taped/re-taped after registration.

### Time out + additional meds
* Clarify intraop goals for: ETCO2, BP. Typically, neurosurgical team will want a normal ETCO2 and SBP <140 for these cases.
* Confirm additional meds: antibiotics (commonly ancef), anti-epileptics (discuss if any are due), steroids (decadron dose), mannitol (not typically administered).

### Maintenance
* **Infusions: REMEMBER that you CANNOT bring infusion pumps into the MRI scanner room!!** You will be able to run infusions from the control room, but the pump must be kept in the control room. The infusion/extension tubing will need to be long enough to reach through a port in the MRI door and to the patient’s IV when they are fully in the scanner.
  * **Before induction**: ensure you have a set of pumps in the OR AND a set in the MRI control room. Double check that the tubing will reach from the control room to the inside of the MRI scanner. Confirm with the MRI tech before you enter the MRI scanner room and ask for help if needed.
  * If you need to move an infusion pump from the OR to the MRI control room, you must walk it through the outside hallway (you can ask circulator or other OR/MRI staff to help you) – never carry it through the MRI scanner room!
* There is no neuromonitoring, so you can run sevo or propofol as a primary anesthetic.
* **Recommended Maintenance Plan** (always use your clinical judgment):
    * Use sevoflurane as the primary agent: simplest option because it does not require an infusion pump or tubing, and you can dose it based on MAC (you will not have a sedline in the MRI and patients on anti-epileptics may have an increased anesthetic requirement)
    * Use Remi for pain: this will also help keep the patient still in the MRI scanner (you will not have a TOF monitor, the patient will be in pins, and you will have limited ability to bolus fent/dilaudid during MRI scanning periods), and it will help facilitate a smooth wake up and an immediate postop neuro exam.
* Maintain paralysis in the operating room. This is always a challenge when you transition to the MRI because you will not have a TOF monitor.

### BP management
* **Pressor**: norepi is usually the pressor of choice to counteract any bradycardia caused by remi.
* **Anti-HTN**: have an anti-hypertensive bolus ready for emergence (typically nicardipine 200mcg/ml). Discuss BP goals with surgical team (often goal SBP <140 to avoid intracranial bleeding).
### IV Fluids
Plasmalyte is the fluid of choice for neuro cases. Aim for euvolemia and avoid giving excessive IV fluid to help minimize risk of cerebral edema.
### Emergence & Extubation
* You will move the patient from the MRI scanner to the MRI induction/emergence room at the end of the surgery (adjacent to the control room). The neurosurgical team will close the burr hole incision in the emergence room before you extubate.
* Ensure paralytic is fully reversed – there should be a TOF monitor in the emergence room.
* Limit use of long-acting opioids: the neurosurgical team usually injects local anesthetic around the (small) incision, so postop pain is typically mild. Consider waking up on remi and then titrating additional pain meds once patient is awake, after the neurosurgical team has completed an initial postop neuro exam.
  * Can consider waking up on low-dose remi (~0.02mcg/kg/m) for young/robust patients. 	
* Avoid coughing and bucking: this can increase ICP and cause intracerebral bleeding.
* Ensure a purposeful response from your patient prior to extubation (ex open eyes, hand squeeze, etc).

### Things to consider
Things to consider when moving from the OR To the MRI Scanner:

* **Plan ahead** – have a plan about how you will transition from the OR to the MRI scanner: know which monitors you need to remember to remove, what infusions you will need in the control room, and how you will move your stuff (ex phone) to the control room (do not leave it in your pocket!!!). Consider writing things down if you might forget to do something.
* Call/text your attending when it is time to move to the MRI scanner.
* See monitoring section about swapping over to MRI monitors and remember to remove any non-compatible monitors/stickers on the patient.
* Participate in a full MRI safety time out with the entire OR team prior to entering the MRI scanner, including a metal detector wand scan.
* Ask someone to transport your belongings (ie phone!!) through the outer hallway to the MRI control room for you. Check your pockets before you start rolling the bed to the MRI room.
* There are a lot of moving parts to remember in this moment and you will often feel rushed. Take a deep breath and double check the patient, yourself, and ensure you followed your plan. Remind others not to rush. Safety is the priority.

### Additional Intraop considerations
* **Intraoperative seizures**: rare in these cases because general anesthesia raises seizure threshold. However, there is always a risk for seizure, so be prepared to immediately manage it.
  * **Communicate** with the surgical team immediately if you start to see seizure-like movements (may be focal rhythmic movements or generalized shaking).
  * **Propofol bolus**: start with a 1mg/kg bolus. Give additional bolus(es) as needed to stop seizure. Propofol is the first line treatment for these cases, not ice cold saline (adequate volume and spread of ice cold saline cannot be accomplished through a burr hole). Consider benzodiazepines as second-line agent. Keppra or fosphenytoin can also be considered (give fosphenytoin slowly!).
  * **Monitor hemodynamics**: Seizures can trigger sympathetic and/or parasympathetic changes, so keep an eye on the vitals.
    * Beware of hypotension after propofol bolus(es).
    * ETCO2 may rise after a large generalized tonic clonic seizure – consider temporarily raising the minute ventilation.
    * Lactate levels may also rise – ensure euvolemia and consider a small fluid bolus (discuss with neurosurgical team); avoid hypervolemia.
  * Once the seizure has subsided, check **patient positioning**: ensure neck is neutral in pins, ensure limbs are supported (arms/legs can slide off bed), pressure points remain padded, etc.
  * Discuss additional dose(s) of anti-epileptic medications with the surgical team.
  * Remember that your patient may be in a post-ictal state at the time of emergence. Post-ictal symptoms can include paralysis, sleepiness, and inability to follow commands. Immediately discuss any concerns with the neurosurgical team and do not extubate the patient until they meet extubation criteria.
* **Glucose**: Remember to check intraop glucose in patients with potential for hyperglycemia. The risk will be increased when steroids are administered.
* **Postop neuro exam**: The neurosurgical team will want to do a neuro exam after extubation, so avoid long-acting meds that can interfere with mental status (example dexmedetomidine, dilaudid, +/-versed) until after neuro exam is completed.
* **Delayed Emergence**: If the patient is not waking up in a timely manner, follow the SNACC guidelines for delayed emergence (confirm paralytic reversal, check glucose, ABG, temp, and sedline). Discuss a postop head CT with surgical team to rule out stroke, bleed, etc (the patient will have presumably just had a normal MRI though). Consider postictal state if patient had an intraop seizure.
* Do **not** order meperidine for neuro patients going to PACU.

## Postop Considerations
* Patients typically go to ICU postoperatively for frequent neuro exams.
* **Handoff** to ICU or PACU must include (in addition to a standard handoff):
  * Description of the patient’s baseline neurological status (this will help prevent an unnecessary code stroke).
  * Description of patient’s seizures (should also be documented in preop note).
  * Any perioperative seizure activity
  * Date/time of last anti-epileptic medication dose and when next dose is due.

## MRI safety
Please leave any unnecessary belongings at home or in a locker: do not bring bags, fanny packs, smart watches, diaries, or anything else you do not absolutely need.
