# Asleep Motor Mapping
**Triple Motor Mapping During Asleep Craniotomies**

Asleep craniotomies for tumor resections are one of the most common neurosurgical procedures that we do.
From a surgical standpoint, the goal is to resect as much of the tumor as possible while limiting damage to functional brain tissue, particularly the motor cortex.
This is achieved using a technique called triple motor mapping in conjunction with neuronavigation and 5-ALA.

Triple motor mapping includes transcranial or transcortical motor evoked potentials (MEPs), bipolar cortical mapping, and monopolar subcortical mapping.
Similar to spine cases, MEPs are performed with corkscrew electrodes in the skull and peripheral needle or sticker electrodes used to monitor descending motor signals.
Occasionally a motor strip will be placed directly on the cortex instead of corkscrew electrodes.
Bipolar cortical stimulation is done using a bipolar probe (aka “The Ojemann”) to deliver an electric current directly to the brain while monitoring for EMG response peripherally.  These two techniques are employed to find a safe entry point through the cortex to access the tumor.

Once safely through the cortex, the surgeons will begin tumor resection.
Throughout resection, they will continue to monitor MEPs, and will use both bipolar and monopolar stimulation to avoid damaging functional brain tissue around the tumor.
Unlike bipolar stimulation, monopolar stimulation sends the electric current down the brain to a grounding pad on the patient’s leg.
If stimulation is in close proximity to subcortical motor tracts, it will elicit a peripheral response.
Once this occurs, the neuromonitoring team will find the minimal amplitude required to elicit the response (aka “the threshold”).
This threshold correlates to distance from the subcortical motor tracts (this is conceptually similar to using electrical stimulation during regional anesthesia).
Being able to estimate this distance allows for maximal tumor resection with minimal damage to surrounding functional brain tissue. 

Why is triple motor mapping relevant to anesthesiologists? There are two primary reasons: because many of our anesthetics can interfere with monitoring and because electrical stimulation to the brain can trigger seizures.  Because the amplitude of the electrical stimulation during monopolar mapping correlates with distance from motor tracts, anesthetics that increase the required amplitude will falsely increase the estimated distance and limit tumor resection.
For this reason, we use nitrous oxide (typically 50-70%) balanced with sevoflurane to achieve 1 MAC as the primary anesthetic during asleep motor mapping.
This is used in conjunction with high dose remi (0.2mcg/kg/m) to minimize the risk of movement or recall.
Although we do not have any blinded studies directly comparing the use nitrous/sevo versus propofol, it has been the experience of the surgeons that nitrous is more favorable. (Author’s note: I have seen a 1-2mA difference when we have compared nitrous/sevo and propofol intraop.
Because resection is typically stopped around 5mA, this may be significant.)
