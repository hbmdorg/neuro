# What is ECoG? 
In a standard electroencephalogram (EEG), 21 electrodes are positioned over the scalp according to a standardized configuration to record the brain’s electrical activity.
Normal EEG waveforms are characterized by several frequency bands, including alpha (8–12 Hz), beta (13–30 Hz), theta (4–7 Hz), and delta (<4 Hz).
Patients with epilepsy may also exhibit interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs), commonly referred to as “spikes,” which result from the synchronous firing of neurons within epileptic foci.
Although EEG is a valuable tool for diagnosing and differentiating various forms of epilepsy, scalp-based recordings have inherent limitations.
In particular, the skull attenuates electrical signals, reducing the ability of scalp electrodes to detect small electrical discharges and activity originating from deep brain structures.

Electrocorticography (ECoG) is similar to electroencephalography (EEG) but involves the placement of recording electrodes directly on the cortical surface beneath the dura.
It is commonly used to guide epilepsy surgery.
Because ECoG requires a craniotomy, electrode placement is generally limited to the exposed cortical region within the craniotomy window.
However, positioning electrodes directly on the brain provides higher-fidelity recordings and improves the detection of interictal epileptiform discharges and electrical activity that may be difficult to detect with scalp EEG, including activity arising from deeper structures such as the hippocampus.

ECoG electrodes can also be strategically positioned and repositioned intraoperatively to optimize coverage of suspected epileptogenic regions, including the mesial temporal lobe.
During surgery, a specialized EEG/ECoG team typically remains present in the operating room to interpret recordings, assist with electrode position, and identify and localize seizure activity.
In the absence of an actual seizure, the frequency and spatial distribution of interictal epileptiform discharges can provide valuable information for localizing epileptogenic tissue. 

Anesthetic agents can significantly influence ECoG recordings and may alter the detection or localization of epileptiform activity.
Therefore, the selection and administration of anesthetic agents should be carefully considered.
Remifentanil and dexmedetomidine do not affect EEG or ECoG recordings, so are often the primary agents for awake cases.
Nitrous is often added for asleep cases, although this varies by institution.
